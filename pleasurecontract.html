1 The question I wanted to answer

By 2026, getting AI to write some code was nothing special. I wanted to try something harder. Could one designer, working with AI agents, take a whole game to the point where it plays, gets tested and is ready to hand over? A card roguelike is a strict exam. It is full of rules, numbers and content, and when any of them is wrong, players feel it in the first fight.

The story is set in Seoul after midnight, when a second city appears to people carrying invisible debts. VIP booths turn into altars, corporate contracts bind souls, and idol agency deals trap spirits. You play a young man pulled into one of those debts. Survive three nights, and find out who wrote your name in the Night Board’s ledger.

2 Every card should make you hesitate

Combat follows the Slay the Spire rhythm players already know: draw, spend energy, attack, block, pick a reward. I kept that part familiar on purpose, so all the tension could come from two resources I added. Affection makes your cards hit harder, but every ally you favor makes the others jealous. Contract hands you the strongest cards in the game and drains your health every turn you carry it. Power always arrives with a bill.

3 Let the enemies tell the story

Each chapter has its own enemies, elite fights and corruption modifiers that bend the rules. I wanted every boss to tell the story through its mechanics, so players feel the theme in their hands before they read it in dialogue.

Chapter 1 · Mirror Backstage
The idol industry, where people are packaged and priced. The boss, Mirror Host, copies the last card you played. The harder you fight the system, the faster it learns you.
Chapter 2 · Chaebol Banquet
Money and debt, where people become collateral. VP Han is a contract that walks. He turns your high affection into new contract and forces a choice: keep borrowing your allies’ power, or give up strength now to protect them.
Chapter 3 · Moonlit Boardroom
The final judgment. The Chairwoman of Night attacks whatever you leaned on most: your allies if affection is high, you if contract is high, your hand if your deck got greedy.
Corruption modifiers
Random rules for each run. Torn Night lowers thresholds, Expensive Desire doubles the cost of strong cards, Mirror Tax punishes skipping, Confession Hour rewards purifying.

4 A relationship web that bites back

Six characters, each tracked across seven values: affection, jealousy, trust, secret progress, route stage, betrayal risk and story flags. Lean on one ally for too long and the others notice. Push jealousy past its threshold and they confront you, in combat. Your party has three slots, a main partner, a side partner and support, so every lineup is also a statement about who you trust.

5 Content lives in data

None of the story is hard-coded. Cards, relics, enemies, character events, recruitment scenes and confrontations all live in JSON. I could change a number or rewrite a scene without touching logic, and agents had clear slots to fill when they added content.

  • 144 cards, plus 12 synergy cards that trigger when specific pairs of characters fight together
  • One map per chapter, with fights, elites, events, confession rooms, shops, contract altars and a boss
  • 22 relics, 19 enemies, and branching visual-novel scenes in English and Chinese
  • Corruption modifiers change the rules on every run
  • Affection buys power and Contract buys risk, so every card pick is a trade

6 It looked finished

Late in the project the game ran. Cards, enemies and story were all in place, in two languages. Before preparing a Steam release I gave it one full check-up, and found several places where “looks finished” and “is finished” had quietly drifted apart.

19 of 22 relics told Chinese players something different from what the code actually did.

Silver Room Key is a good example. In English and in code, it gives every block card +1 block. The Chinese text promised 2 armor at the start of each turn. Each file looked fine on its own; the error only showed when you put them side by side. Agents had written them in separate passes, and nothing was checking whether they described the same thing.

  • The story file was missing its opening brace. Worse, the data loader never complained about broken files. It logged a warning, returned an empty list and let the game boot, so bad data looked like a design bug.
  • Some interface text used the Chinese sentence itself as its lookup key. Edit one character of copy and the lookup would silently break.
  • The refactor plan said Main.gd would end up under 500 lines. It is still over 12,000. Agents are very good at adding code and almost never take it out on their own.

7 The checks I added

Asking agents to “be more careful” doesn’t scale. So I made mistakes loud instead.

Pin the numbers first
Before moving any combat math, I wrote 11 golden-sample checks covering attacks, block, armor, vulnerable and frail, poison and bleed ticks, and contract pressure. Only then did the pure math move into CombatSystem. If a number changes, a test fails.
Fail loudly
Loaders now stop with the exact file and reason instead of returning an empty list. I broke a data file on purpose to make sure it really does.
Reconcile the languages
Every relic description was rewritten with the code as the source of truth. A script then checked that each Chinese description names the stats the relic really carries. Zero mismatches across all 22.
Stable keys, safe saves
Interface text moved to stable English keys. Save files are versioned and written atomically, so a crash can’t leave half a save behind.

What I took from it
Agents wrote most of these fifteen thousand lines. Not once did they tell me the Chinese relic text was wrong. That part of the job stayed mine: deciding what to build, writing the edges tightly enough to check, and then playing the pickiest player I can imagine. If I started over, the checks would come before the content.
