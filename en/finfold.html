01

Overview

One product update
seven rewrites

After shipping a product update, I wanted to post it on X, Xiaohongshu, WeChat, LinkedIn, Reddit and Product Hunt. Copying took seconds. Adapting took forever. A chatbot could draft, but every channel meant explaining the product, the audience and the platform’s unwritten rules all over again. One launch turned into seven rounds of prompt roulette, and the time I saved went straight into checking.

Each time I rewrote it, I noted what that pass was actually fixing. Two kinds of work kept showing up. Names, prices, dates and product limits could not change by a single word. Openings, structure, tone and image sizes had to change for every channel. Everything I designed in Finfold afterwards deals with how those two kinds of content live together.

05

Insights & opportunities

Everyone re-explained their brand
no one wanted to post blind

The same lines kept coming up in interviews. Every session started with re-introducing the brand. Every channel needed its own pass. And at the last step, nobody wanted a machine to press publish for them. Those three remarks became three design principles.

01 / “I start from scratch every time”

Introduce the brand once

Brand memory and rules come along to every session

02 / “Every channel needs its own pass”

Make channel differences visible

Switch channels and edit each draft on its own

03 / “I want to press the last button”

Keep publish in human hands

Review first, then hand off, with consequences visible

08

Design decisions

What AI can carry
and the step that stays human

Getting AI to draft is no longer the hard part. The hard part is deciding what to hand over and which step must stay with a person. This chapter is a handful of those calls, including two things I deleted myself.

01 / Context design

Make brand context visible and editable

Early on, the brand check ran last. All eleven drafts were generated before the system warned that the tone might be off. The warning was right, it just came too late: people had already edited, and starting over felt like punishment. So I moved the rules before generation. How the brand is spelled, which numbers can’t change, which words are off-limits all go into context first, then get checked again afterwards.

Saved voice principle
Clear, credible, without overpromising
Joey Zhao · Person + brand

The design decision

Context you repeat goes into memory; each task’s source stays on the workbench. Brand information lasts for months while sources change every time, so they live in separate places, and people can always see what the AI was working from.

03 / Honest states

I deleted two things
that made the demo look better

Neither change shows up in a screenshot, and the demo got slightly worse. I made them anyway. People spend years building an account, and one post with a wrong fact lands on them, not on us.

01

A row of pretty fake numbers

The first overview screen showed 42 posts queued, 11 in review, 18 scheduled. It made the product look mature. The numbers were demo data, so the whole row went. An honest empty state is better than a borrowed full one.

02

A fallback that always succeeded

When the model failed, the system quietly slipped in a template and still showed success. Demos ran smoothly, and users had no way to tell which drafts the model wrote and which were filler. Now a failed card says why, can be retried on its own, and every successful result stays put.

Make the starting point obvious

The Agent opens with a prompt and common tasks. Supporting panels appear when needed, so the first screen stays approachable.

Keep frequent actions next to the result

Editing and copying stay within reach. Secondary controls step back so the content remains the focus.

01

Honest progress

Recovery

03

Human review

04

Six channels at a time

Running all fourteen channels at once looks impressive, but waiting and failures rise together. Six is closer to a real launch batch, and still something a person can review properly.

09

Three tasks

Testing skipped the smooth path on purpose. Two of the three tasks were about what happens after something goes wrong: fixing a flawed draft, and leaving halfway then coming back. That is where AI products most often lose trust.

The habit this project
broke for me

AI products are easy to design for the demo. The happy path is the easiest thing to draw and the easiest way to fool yourself. This time I spent most of my hours on the moments a demo never shows: the model is slow, a draft fails, someone leaves and comes back. None of that makes it on stage, but it’s exactly where people decide whether to trust the product with their account.
