01

Overview

Underneath seven rewrites
lived two opposite rules

After shipping a release, I set out to share it across X, Xiaohongshu, WeChat, LinkedIn, Reddit and Product Hunt. Copying took three seconds; adapting took hours. A generic chatbot could generate paragraphs, yet every channel meant re-explaining the product, the audience and each community’s unwritten rules from scratch. One launch turned into seven rounds of prompt roulette—and every minute saved on drafting went straight back into fact-checking.

On each pass, I logged what I was actually fixing. By the seventh rewrite, the work split cleanly in two: facts—product names, pricing, dates and capability limits—could not drift by a single character; form—hooks, pacing, tone and image ratios—had to reshape for every feed. Every architectural and state decision in Finfold comes down to holding fixed facts and fluid expression in one workspace.

03

Competitive analysis

One tool guards the voice
another guards the calendar

Look at the strongest tools on either side of the workflow: Jasper anchors consistent brand voice upstream, while Buffer organizes multi-channel calendars and handoffs downstream. Yet for solo founders and lean teams, the hardest stretch sits right in the middle—turning one raw product update into five or six channel-native drafts without distorting a single fact. Users still carried that middle stretch by hand across chat tabs and docs.

05

Insights & opportunities

Tired of re-briefing the bot
unwilling to let it post blind

When we laid interview notes side by side, three remarks kept echoing: having to re-introduce the brand in every fresh chat, rewriting structure channel by channel, and refusing to let an algorithm press publish on a real account. Those three human boundaries became Finfold’s three design principles.

01 / “I start from scratch every time”

Introduce the brand once

Store positioning, voice and banned terms in visible memory for every run

02 / “Every channel needs its own pass”

Put channel differences in plain view

Replace scrolling chat threads with independent per-channel cards

03 / “I want to press the last button”

Keep the publish button human

AI drafts and checks; human review and publishing handoff stay explicit

06

Architecture & prototyping

Six scattered chores into three steps
give the room back to the draft

Previously, one launch fractured into six scattered chores across chat tabs, docs and spreadsheets. Why not build another chat UI? Because six channels produce six parallel objects with independent states—a linear chat stream buries earlier drafts the moment you revise.

I compressed the workflow into three stages backed by persistent brand memory, and rebalanced the viewport to match. Our first prototype split source, channels and outputs into three equal columns, squeezing the draft—the exact text a human must proofread word by word—into a narrow strip. Merging setup controls on the left gave the draft the room careful editing demands.

UI & design system

Quiet the chrome

Many AI tools lean on neon gradients and glowing borders to advertise “intelligence.” Inside an editing workspace, visual theatrics fight the reader’s eye. 08

Design decisions

Where AI carries the load
where human judgment holds the line

Getting an LLM to generate fluent paragraphs is effortless now. What separates a toy from a workhorse is where you draw the boundaries: when constraints step in, how structure bends without warping facts, and how the UI behaves when models fail. Here are those concrete calls—including two “demo pleasers” I deleted myself.

01 / Pre-generation context

Move brand rules upstream—before a word is written

Early on, the brand check ran last: all eleven drafts finished generating before the system warned that tone might be off. Accurate warning, terrible timing—people had already waited and started editing, making a redo feel like punishment. So I moved rules upstream: exact brand spelling, immutable numbers and banned words enter context before generation, then run a verification pass after.

Saved voice principle
Clear, credible, without overpromising
Joey Zhao · Live personal + brand profile

Why separate memory from brief

Brand positioning and banned words hold steady for months; launch notes change every session. Splitting persistent memory from the per-task brief stops repetitive pasting and keeps the AI’s active rules inspectable.

03 / Honest states

I deleted two features
that made stage demos look smoother

Neither cut flatters a static screenshot, and both made live demos riskier. I cut them anyway. People spend years building trust on an account; if a flattering UI masks a flawed draft, the damage lands on them in public.

01

A row of flattering fake metrics

The first overview screen showed “42 queued, 11 in review, 18 scheduled.” It looked mature on stage—except the numbers were hardcoded demo filler. To a real new user, pre-filled counts immediately spark confusion and distrust. Deleting them stung for a second, yet an honest empty state beats borrowed maturity every time.

02

A silent fallback that faked success

Early on, when a model failed, the system quietly slipped in a generic template and still marked the card green. Demos ran without a hitch—and real users had no way to tell genuine drafts from canned filler. Now a failed card states why and retries on its own, leaving every successful sibling card intact.

Give first-time users an obvious foothold

The Agent opens with one prompt bar and concrete starter tasks; secondary panels unfold on demand rather than crowding screen one.

Keep review actions right beside the words

Edit and copy sit immediately on the card while secondary knobs step back—keeping eyes on the text under review.

Streaming progress

03

Explicit review gate

Separate drafting, human checking and publishing handoff so every action’s consequence is predictable

04

14 channels supported, capped at 6 per run

Finfold supports 14 channels, yet caps each batch at six. Running all 14 looks flashy on stage, but latency and partial timeouts compound—and human proofreading sharpens on six drafts where it blurs on fourteen. The cap protects system reliability and review quality together.

09

Usability testing

Skip the happy-path demo
test where AI workflows break

Asking whether a prompt returns text only tests the model; testing what happens after things go sideways tests the UX. Out of three usability tasks, two deliberately targeted where AI tools lose trust: catching and fixing a flawed claim in one channel variant, and leaving mid-flow then picking right back up.

TASK 01

Turn one product update
into two channel drafts

Verified first-run comprehension, brief input and channel selection

TASK 02

Spot a flawed claim
and revise a single card

Verified per-card editing ergonomics and version isolation across channels

TASK 03

Leave mid-session
and resume without loss

Verified content-kit retrieval and interrupted draft recovery

The design habit
Finfold broke for me

Designing AI products makes it dangerously easy to optimize for the three-minute stage demo. One prompt, eleven instant cards—the happy path is effortless to mock up and flattering to present. On Finfold, my hardest hours went into moments that never appear on stage: a slow model, a single failed channel, a tab closed mid-edit. Nobody claps for recovery states, yet they are the exact moments users decide whether to trust you with an account they spent years building.
