Whatever Happened to Molecule No. 55?

A lung drug candidate, 18 months and a $2.6 million discovery budget. Follow one molecule from a chemist’s shortlist to a hospital, and see which parts AI can hurry along.

Joey Zhao2026.11.208 min read

The chemists went with number 55. In Insilico Medicine’s account, they had picked 79 molecular designs to make in the lab. The one they carried forward eventually got a name, rentosertib. By September 2026, a patient in China had taken it in a phase 3 trial. Team account Phase 3 announcement

What I like about this story is the rather ordinary question underneath it. A chemist has two hands and a finite number of mornings at the bench. Which compound gets made tomorrow? That’s one place where AI for Science, or AI4S, can change the course of drug discovery. A human could have drawn molecule 55. Whether anyone would have got around to making it before the money ran out is another matter.

What is a year and a half worth?

I put two of Insilico’s accounts side by side. From looking for a target to choosing a candidate for preclinical development, the team spent about 18 months on this project, with a reported budget of roughly $2.6 million. The company gives two and a half to four years as a typical duration for traditional early discovery. In the same units, that’s 30 to 48 months against 18. The difference on paper is a year to two and a half years. Company budget disclosure Time benchmark

A year is a long time for a lab to wait for an answer. Even if the answer is disappointing, learning it a year earlier leaves time to try something else. That’s the prospect I’d bet on before promising an earlier date at the pharmacy. Nobody paid a second team to run this exact project from scratch without AI. We don’t know how long that team would have taken.

What about the money? The $2.6 million was the company’s budget for discovery. In a later case study it estimated the cost of that stage at about a tenth of the traditional figure, without publishing a like-for-like breakdown. There would still be hospital bills once trials began. For a closer look at one expense AI can cut, I’d turn to a different experiment, one where researchers could actually compare the two approaches. Company comparison

Imagine taking each molecule in a library, fitting its shape into a little pocket on a protein and paying for the computing time to score it. A research team tried doing the expensive calculation on a small sample first. A model learned from those examples and helped pick which compounds were worth a closer look. When the researchers checked against molecules already confirmed as hits in experiments, they recovered more than 80% of them for about one fourteenth of the computing cost. Yang et al.

For a calculation that used to cost fourteen dollars, think one dollar, about 93% less for that particular bill. The shortcut also missed some good compounds. If missing one is costly enough, I’d still pay the fourteen. But suppose a library was so expensive to explore that nobody was going to look there at all. A dollar for a first pass is a different proposition. This was a separate screening study. Its computing costs and the $2.6 million rentosertib discovery budget belong on different receipts.

Where do you start with a scarred lung?

The disease in this story is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Look at it from across the room and you can see hazy markings on a chest X-ray. Look through a microscope and you can see dense scar tissue crowding the fine spaces that used to hold air. A lung has to stretch each time someone draws breath. Scars make that harder.

A pathologist can point to the damage. A chemist needs something to act on. The team gathered fifteen public datasets from blood and lung tissue. Sick cells keep different genes busy from healthy ones. The catch is that a gene can be busy for two quite different reasons. It might be helping cause an injury, or the cell might be trying to repair the damage.

The team used models to compare these changes against known relationships between proteins and findings in the literature. They narrowed the search to a family of proteins that small molecules might be able to reach. A protein called TNIK rose to the top. When they looked at a separate set of data that distinguished individual cells, TNIK was more active in a type involved in scarring. That was a reason to try acting on it. When you see “target” in a drug paper, this is what the researchers hope to affect. Ren et al.

A molecule that binds can still make a poor pill

The computer had a three-dimensional structure for TNIK. It could propose small molecules that might fit into it. Chemists picked structures they had a reasonable chance of making and tested them. Some of the early compounds already bound quite tightly. If binding had been the whole assignment, they’d have had a handsome figure for the report. Ren et al.

Then somebody has to swallow the thing. Several early compounds dissolved poorly. Tests with material from human and mouse livers suggested that they would also be broken down too quickly. A pill has to dissolve before its active ingredient can be absorbed, and enough of that ingredient has to last long enough to reach the lung. Those measurements gave the chemists something specific to improve in the next round. With the eventual candidate they could go a step further, to cells derived from a donor with the disease. Under a microscope, a bright green marker grew fainter. You can see the difference in the two images below. Ren et al.

It’s a long way from a dimmer patch of green to easier breathing. I like those images for a more modest reason. A scientist who started with a drawing now had an observation that another lab could check, and a reason to decide whether the next experiment deserved funding.

The compound I nearly let go missing

At Revvity Signals, I worked on how scientists ask questions, filter results and get back to a record. I know molecule 55 through the papers. The experiment I can speak to personally involved a different protein, CDK2, and a public file with 94 activity records. I used it to check our software. Which compounds could cut the measured activity in half at a very low concentration? ChEMBL

The column for that concentration was called IC₅₀. The unit, nM, measures concentration. In this assay, a lower number means less compound was needed. I set the cutoff at 10 nM. Signals AI gave me 43 records. I checked the identifiers and values, and all 43 matched. Then I moved the line to 100 nM. On the chart, the three bins below it held 1, 42 and 35 records. Try the addition yourself. The accompanying text said 77.

One short. What bothers me is how good a reason I had to trust it after the first answer. I asked the assistant to go back to the file. This time it got 78. Another row said “>1000”. The assistant took that to mean the experimenters had only tested up to 1000. The record said the result was greater than 1000. To find out how far the experimenters actually went, I’d have to read their methods.

So I’m willing to give an unglamorous source table quite a bit of screen space. An assistant can do the first pass over dozens of records for me. If a number looks odd, I want one click back to that row. That saves me having to do the entire first pass again, which is a fairly practical definition of time well spent. My project notes

Some things take a full year

In the phase 2a trial, 71 people with pulmonary fibrosis were randomly assigned to four groups. They received different dosing schedules or placebo for twelve weeks. At a lung function test, you fill your lungs and blow into the mouthpiece until you have nothing left. The volume of air that comes out is called forced vital capacity. It takes much longer to explain the name than to understand the movement. Xu et al.

At twelve weeks, the group taking 60 mg once daily could blow out an average of 98.4 mL more than at the start. The placebo group averaged 20.3 mL less. What I wanted to know was whether someone could walk to the shops without stopping to rest. The researchers did measure how far people could walk in six minutes. Changes in walking distance were similar across groups. It was a small, short trial. That question is still worth asking. Xu et al.

The main question here was safety. Sixteen of the 71 participants stopped treatment early. Reasons for stopping included liver problems and diarrhoea. The phase 3 trial is looking at how quickly lung capacity falls over fifty-two weeks. You might gain a year choosing a molecule at the start and still have a good reason to wait every week of that year at the other end. Xu et al. GENESIS-IPF-3

I keep thinking about the experiments that never get funded. Molecule 55 made it to a hospital. Someone else’s number 55 may still be a drawing on a chemist’s computer. If that chemist opens next year’s budget and finds they can afford to make it after all, I’d count that as a pretty good day.
