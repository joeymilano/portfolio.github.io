How Does AI Help Make a Drug? From One Molecule to a Lung Function Test

About 18 months and a $2.6 million discovery budget bought a team one molecule worth pursuing. After that came the animal studies and the trials in people, and none of them could be skipped. Designing software for scientists at Revvity Signals taught me to ask a second question. Would I trust the shortlist enough to take it to the lab tomorrow?

Joey Zhao2026.11.2017 min read

Chemists picked 79 structures off a screen to make in a lab. Number 55 was the one they took forward, according to Insilico Medicine. What had begun as a shape you could turn on a computer acquired a name, rentosertib. A few years later, someone with lung disease actually swallowed it. In September 2026, the first participant received a dose in a Chinese phase 3 trial.

What I like about this story is the rather ordinary question underneath it. A chemist has two hands and a finite number of mornings at the bench. Which compound gets made tomorrow? That’s one place where AI for Science, or AI4S, can change which experiments go ahead before anyone takes a dose. A human could have drawn molecule 55. Whether anyone would have got around to making it before the money ran out is another matter.

What is a year and a half worth?

I put two of Insilico’s accounts side by side. From looking for a target to choosing molecule 55, the team spent about 18 months on this project, with a reported budget of roughly $2.6 million. Their own benchmark is two and a half to four years for a traditional team to reach the same point. In the same units, that’s 30 to 48 months against 18. The difference on paper is a year to two and a half years.

A year is a long time for a lab to wait for an answer. Even if the answer is disappointing, learning it a year earlier leaves time to try something else. That’s the prospect I’d bet on before promising an earlier date at the pharmacy. Nobody paid a second team to run this exact project the traditional way, so nobody knows how long that team would have taken.

Insilico puts that $2.6 million at about a tenth of a traditional discovery budget. It hasn’t published an itemized comparison. The hospital trials come on a separate bill. Is there one expense for which researchers have run both approaches and can compare them directly? There is.

Imagine taking each molecule in a library, fitting its shape into a little pocket on a protein and paying for the computing time to score it. The work is like trying a huge keyring against a single lock, and every attempt costs money. A research team tried doing the expensive calculation on a small sample first. A model learned from those examples and helped pick which compounds were worth a closer look. When the researchers checked against a list of molecules already confirmed as hits in experiments, the new approach recovered more than 80% of them for about one fourteenth of the computing cost.

For a calculation that used to cost fourteen dollars, think one dollar, about 93% less for that particular bill. The shortcut also missed some good compounds. That is one reason I find the result credible. If missing one is costly enough, I’d still pay the fourteen. But suppose a library was so expensive to explore that nobody was going to look there at all. A dollar for a first pass is a different proposition. This was a separate screening study. Its computing costs and the $2.6 million rentosertib discovery budget belong on different receipts.

What happens before the first volunteer takes a pill?

Those 18 months cover the first three steps on the map below. They end with one molecule chosen, which in the trade is called a preclinical candidate. The search up to that point is drug discovery. Steps four and five are preclinical development, where the question gets harder. Before the first volunteer takes this pill, how much do we know about the risk?

Are we making aspirin or insulin?

Aspirin is a familiar example of a small-molecule drug. Its active ingredient has a well-defined chemical structure and can be made through chemical reactions. Rentosertib is a small molecule too. Now think of the human insulin used to treat diabetes. It’s a protein. Manufacturers typically use bacterial or yeast cells to produce it. Therapeutic antibodies belong to the same family, called biologics. A chemist working on a small molecule might swap out a group of atoms. A team designing a protein drug also has to consider how a chain of amino acids will fold. Keep that distinction in mind. It comes up again when we get to monkeys.

Where do you start with a scarred lung?

The disease in this story is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Look at it from across the room and you can see hazy markings on a chest X-ray. Look through a microscope and you can see dense scar tissue crowding the fine spaces that used to hold air. A lung has to stretch each time someone draws breath. Scars make that harder.

A pathologist can point to the damage. A chemist needs something to act on. Is there something within reach that, if you interfere with it, slows the scarring? The team gathered fifteen public datasets, four from blood and eleven from lung tissue. Sick cells keep different genes busy from healthy ones. The catch is that a gene can be busy for two quite different reasons. It might be helping cause an injury, or the cell might be trying to repair the damage.

The team stacked several kinds of evidence. Models compared gene activity in patients and healthy people, then checked it against known relationships between proteins and published research. Filters kept only proteins that small molecules could reach, were novel and, ideally, had a known structure. On the company’s blog, the model handed over 20 targets for validation. TNIK ranked first in that run.

Ranking first didn’t settle it. The team turned to a second dataset that could resolve individual cells. In patient lungs, TNIK was more active than in healthy lungs in the cells that deposit collagen and stiffen the lung. The researchers also switched TNIK off in cells, and the cells changed in much the same way as they did after the drug. When you see “target” in a drug paper, this is what the researchers hope to affect. Only at this point did TNIK qualify.

A molecule that binds can still make a poor pill

A three-dimensional structure for TNIK already existed, so the team had the shape of the keyhole and the models could draw molecules to fit it. Thirty generative models worked in parallel and could see one another’s structures and scores. Designs lost points for being too heavy, too hard to dissolve, unlikely to work as a drug by past experience or too roundabout to make. Chemists then chose on three counts, whether a molecule could be made, whether it was new enough and whether it looked like a drug. Some of the early compounds already bound quite tightly to TNIK. If binding had been the whole assignment, they’d have had a handsome figure for the report.

Then somebody has to swallow the thing. A pill has to dissolve in the gut before the body can absorb it. Enough of it has to survive the liver and stay in the body long enough to reach the lung. The first batches failed here. They dissolved too little, and the liver cleared them too fast.

That started a cycle of optimisation. A model looked at the last round of results and suggested the next set of changes. Chemists made the molecules and tested them. The company chose 79 molecules to make, and number 55 won. Its strength is economy. Very little of it stopped fibroblasts turning into scar-forming cells, and harming the cells themselves took about three thousand times more. The researchers then grew fibroblasts from lung tissue donated by patients and added the chosen molecule. Under a microscope, a bright green marker grew fainter. You can see the difference in the two images below.

It’s a long way from a dimmer patch of green to easier breathing. I like those images for a more modest reason. A scientist who started with a drawing on a computer now had an observation that another lab could check, and a reason to decide whether the next experiment deserved funding.

If it worked in a dish, why look at a mouse lung?

Cells in a dish sit in the drug. A living mouse that swallows it is a different matter. The drug has to pass through the stomach and gut, be absorbed, get past the liver and reach the blood. Then enough of it has to reach the lung. Scar tissue isn’t the work of one cell type either, and it takes weeks. A dish can’t rebuild that.

So animal studies answer two questions that are easy to confuse. Does the drug work? What does it harm? The sections below answer only the first.

The researchers used a model that has been around for decades. On day one, a little bleomycin went into the windpipe. It is a chemotherapy drug that makes mouse lungs scar over slowly. From day seven the mice were dosed by mouth twice a day. One group got the solvent only. Three groups got molecule 55 at 3, 10 or 30 milligrams per kilogram of body weight. Another group got nintedanib, an approved antifibrotic drug, to serve as a yardstick. Four weeks on, the lungs were removed, stained and measured for the share of scarred area.

The higher the dose, the less scar. Fibrotic area fell by more than half in the lowest dose group compared with the solvent group, and by more than three quarters in the middle and high dose groups. Improvement in lung function was on a par with the nintedanib group.

The drug was also tested in a rat inhalation model, a mouse kidney fibrosis model and a rat skin scar model, and fibrosis eased in each. Beyond the scar, the team had to measure where the drug goes in the body and how long it stays. In mice, about 30% of an oral dose reaches the bloodstream.

Efficacy is settled. The next question is whether it does harm.

Before anyone took a dose, which animals were tested for harm?

The main text of the paper has one sentence on toxicology. It says the safety profile was favourable. The details are in the supplement. Each of the three clinical trial protocols carries a summary of the nonclinical work, and that is where the toxicology sits.

The rule is set by regulators together. Before a drug reaches people, repeat-dose toxicity studies have to be run in two mammalian species, one rodent and one non-rodent, and they have to last at least as long as the trial in people that they support. They are run under GLP, Good Laboratory Practice, which sets rules for how a study is designed, recorded and archived. China’s regulator joined the body that writes these rules in 2017 and has followed this one since 2021.

The centrepiece is the 28-day repeat-dose study, one in mice and one in beagle dogs. There was toxicity. It appeared from 60 mg/kg per day in mice and from 30 in dogs, and it grew worse with dose. The first tissues affected were the immune and blood-forming tissues, and the gut.

AI has a limited part here. A model can predict which molecule is harder to absorb or more likely to injure the liver, and that helps a team order its tests. What goes into the application can only be numbers measured in animals.

Why mice and beagle dogs, and no monkeys

That pairing is the usual one for a small molecule, one rodent and one dog. Monkeys are mostly for antibody drugs. An antibody drug recognises a human protein. The matching protein in a mouse or dog is shaped differently, so the antibody often fails to recognise it. Toxicity studies in such animals can be actively misleading. So antibody drugs require a “relevant species”, an animal in which the drug actually works. Monkeys are close relatives of ours. For many antibody drugs the monkey is the only relevant species, so the toxicology runs in monkeys alone. Rentosertib is a small molecule, so it was tested in mice and beagle dogs.

The first human dose was worked out from a mouse

Toxicology has one more direct use. It sets the first dose for people. The protocol used the mouse figure. At 30 mg/kg per day for 28 days, no harm was seen. That is the mouse’s no-observed-adverse-effect level. Converted to a person by body surface area, it is 2.4 mg/kg. A further tenfold safety margin brings it to 0.24 mg/kg. For a 60 kg adult, the ceiling for a single dose is about 15 mg. The phase 1 trial started at 10 mg.

Will animal testing be replaced?

The rules in the US are loosening. At the end of 2022, Congress removed the requirement that a new drug be tested in animals before people, and allowed cell-based assays, organoids and computer models instead. This drug came before that. Its animal studies were finished before anyone received a first dose.

The AI counted 77. Would you take that list to the lab?

In those five steps, AI handed over shortlists, suggested changes and made predictions. People decided, and people checked. Whether a shortlist can be trusted was the question I faced daily while building products.

At Revvity Signals, we discussed giving the AI a tool of its own. But the scientist already had an experiment open. For one question about which compounds deserved another test, would we ask them to open another window, move a file and explain the experiment all over again? I argued for putting the conversation beside the existing record. The file was right there.

Later, I checked the design using a public file from a different project. It contained 94 activity records for a protein called CDK2. I wanted compounds that could halve the measured activity at a low concentration, the value labelled IC₅₀ in the file. At a cutoff of 10 nM, the assistant returned 43 records. I checked every identifier and value. All 43 matched.

I raised the cutoff to 100 nM. The assistant’s summary now said 77 compounds. On the very same screen, the three bars below that cutoff held these counts.

See why I asked for a recount

78 records. I asked the assistant to check the original file and it revised its answer to 78. After getting all 43 records right on the previous question, it had given me every reason to copy down 77.

That is why I gave the chart and the source table space on the screen. On a good day, the assistant goes through dozens of records and hands over a shortlist. When the numbers disagree, the scientist can open the cited file and settle the matter at the row in question. That saves a file transfer and keeps a miscount out of the notes that go to the bench tomorrow.

Why things slow down once the drug reaches people

  1. Phase 0Healthy volunteers receive a tiny dose to show how the drug behaves in the body. The first time anyone received this drug, 8 volunteers in Australia were given 100 micrograms by vein, one hundredth of the phase 1 starting dose
  2. Phase 1A small group helps establish tolerability and how long the drug stays in the body. One rentosertib study in New Zealand enrolled 78 healthy volunteers, with doses climbing from 10 mg in successive cohorts. Some cancer trials enroll patients from the start.
  3. Phase 2In people with the disease, is there an early sign of benefit? What dose makes sense? Safety monitoring continues
  4. Phase 3A larger trial compares benefits and risks against a control group. For rentosertib, that means a planned 320 patients followed for 52 weeks.

There is time to save in the hospital, too. In a small NIH test, AI highlighted the relevant passages in medical records and two clinicians checked patients against trial criteria about 40% faster. It was a small test. A patient’s twelve weeks of follow-up still takes twelve weeks.

The phase 2a trial ran at 21 hospitals in China. Seventy-one people with pulmonary fibrosis were randomly assigned to three dose groups or placebo for twelve weeks. At a lung function test, you fill your lungs and blow into the mouthpiece until you have nothing left. The volume of air that comes out is called forced vital capacity, the measure these trials rely on most.

At twelve weeks, the group taking 60 mg once daily could blow out an average of 98.4 mL more than at the start. The placebo group averaged 20.3 mL less. What I wanted to know was whether someone could walk to the shops without stopping to rest. The researchers did measure how far people could walk in six minutes. Changes in walking distance were similar across groups. With fewer than twenty people in each group and twelve weeks of follow-up, that question is still open.

The main question here was safety. Sixteen of the 71 participants did not finish the twelve weeks, seven of them because of liver injury or abnormal liver function. Compare that with the animal toxicology. The gut was among the first tissues affected there. The liver is not among the target organs listed in the protocol. So one problem showed up in the animals first and the other did not. The phase 3 trial is looking at how quickly lung capacity falls over fifty-two weeks. You might gain a year choosing a molecule at the start and still have a good reason to wait every week of that year at the other end.

After phase 3, the team may be in a position to apply for marketing approval. Regulators weigh the benefits and risks for the intended patients and review manufacturing quality. If the drug is approved, rare harms may only become apparent as more people take it for longer. Reports from patients and clinicians keep coming in, sometimes alongside dedicated phase 4 studies.

The next time I read about a drug that AI made, I’ll check four things
  1. Was the target found by AIRanked out of data by a model, or studied by people for years? The paper says which
  2. How many molecules were made and testedThat number means more than “a million generated”
  3. Which two animals carried the toxicology studies, and for how longIf nobody says, be suspicious
  4. Which phase has it reachedA signal in phase 2 still leaves phase 3 and review behind it

I keep thinking about the experiments that never get funded. Molecule 55 made it to a hospital. Someone else’s number 55 may still be a drawing on a chemist’s computer. If that chemist opens next year’s budget and finds they can afford to make it after all, I’d count that as a pretty good day.
