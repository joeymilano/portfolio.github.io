How Does AI Help Make a Drug?

Following rentosertib from a list of proteins to a lung function test in 71 people, with a detour through my work at Revvity Signals. A case study in AI for Science (AI4S), and in the people who check its answers.

Joey Zhao2026.11.2010 min read

Fill your lungs, close your lips around the mouthpiece and blow until you have nothing left. The volume that comes out is called forced vital capacity, or FVC. It takes a few seconds to measure. In a 2025 paper on a drug designed with the help of AI, it produced the number that stayed with me. After twelve weeks, one group taking a drug candidate called rentosertib could blow out an average of 98.4 millilitres more than they had at the start.

Would someone with that much extra air find a flight of stairs any easier? I wanted to know. The researchers had, in fact, taken the trouble to measure how far people could walk in six minutes. Changes in walking distance were similar across groups. There was a person at the end of each of those measurements, and the instrument and the walk down the corridor told different parts of their story. Phase 2a paper

At Revvity Signals I design software for scientists. I know the stretch of the road that happens on a screen, where someone has a list of compounds and has to decide which ones deserve another afternoon at the bench. I wanted to follow one molecule far enough to see what stands between a choice on a screen and that extra breath.

When a lung changes, where do you start looking?

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis scars the lungs. The delicate walls of the air sacs, across which oxygen passes into the blood, grow thick and stiff in affected areas. “Idiopathic” means the cause is unknown. A pathologist can point to the damage on a slide. A chemist still needs to know what to aim for.

A team at Insilico Medicine started with fifteen public datasets on the disease. Four came from blood and eleven from lung tissue. They held measurements of gene activity. If a gene is unusually active in a diseased lung, it’s tempting to blame it for the damage. But the same observation would make sense if the cells were trying to repair a wound. Paying attention to the wrong signal could lead you to interfere with a repair.

The team used models to follow known relationships between proteins and to weigh those clues alongside changes in gene activity and evidence in the literature. Scientists narrowed the search to kinases, enzymes that can alter the activity of other proteins, with an eye to reaching one with a small molecule. Under those settings, a protein called TNIK came first. A protein you hope to affect with a drug is what researchers mean by a target. Discovery paper

How much lab time would you spend on a first-place finish? I like what the team did next. Measuring gene activity across a piece of lung tissue is a bit like reading one electricity meter for an entire building. The researchers looked at data that separated individual cells. In diseased lungs, they found higher TNIK expression in myofibroblasts, cells involved in laying down scar tissue. Now they had a reason to try intervening and see what happened.

The first molecules ran into a practical problem

The team had a three-dimensional structure for TNIK and could identify a pocket that a small molecule might bind to. A second model proposed chemical structures. Chemists then chose which ones were novel enough to pursue, feasible to make and promising enough to test. Only when somebody made one did a drawing on a screen become something they could put in an assay.

Some early candidates bound tightly to TNIK. They also dissolved poorly, and tests with material from human and mouse livers suggested they would be broken down too quickly. Think about what you are asking of a tablet. Its active ingredient has to dissolve, get absorbed and reach the place it can act before the body clears it. A strong binding result tells you remarkably little about whether it can make that trip. Discovery paper

Those disappointing measurements gave the chemists something concrete to ask of the next round. They worked on how the compounds would be absorbed and metabolised. The candidate they eventually nominated was called INS018_055. Later it acquired a pronounceable name, rentosertib. Tests in patient-derived cells and animal models supplied evidence of antifibrotic activity. Early studies in people examined tolerability and how blood concentrations changed over time. The figure of roughly eighteen months, often quoted for this project, runs from target discovery to nomination of the preclinical candidate. A patient waiting for a treatment would have had some years left on their clock. Discovery paper

This is what interests me about AI for Science, or AI4S. It has serious institutional backing. The European Commission lists €107 million in dedicated funding for its RAISE science pilot in the 2026–27 work programme. What makes science one of the best places to put AI to work, in my view, happens on a smaller scale. A model proposes a structure. A chemist makes it and finds it barely dissolves. The next decision can draw on a fact nobody had before. European Commission

The time I was one compound short

I know the rentosertib work through the papers. The part I can speak to first-hand is what happens when scientists interrogate a result in software. At Signals, I worked on how people ask questions, filter results and get back to the evidence. To check the experience, I used a public ChEMBL assay for a different protein, CDK2. It contained 94 compound activity records. Having the original file beside me meant I could check every answer. Original assay

I first asked for records with an IC₅₀ under 10 nM. IC₅₀ is the concentration needed to cut the measured activity by half under the conditions of that particular experiment. Within the same assay, a smaller value means it took less compound to reach that mark. Nanomolar, abbreviated nM, is the concentration unit. Signals AI returned 43 records. I checked the identifiers and values against the file. All 43 matched.

Then I raised the cutoff to 100 nM. On the chart, the three bins below the new threshold contained 1, 42 and 35 records. You can do the sum yourself.

I got 78. The written summary said 77. I pointed out the discrepancy and asked the assistant to check the original file. It revised the total to 78. What bothers me about that mistake is how thoroughly the system had earned my confidence on the previous question. If I’d only had a paragraph of fluent prose, I’d have had little reason to slow down on this one.

Another row contained an even smaller detail. The notation was “>1000 nM”. The assistant explained that the experiment had tested concentrations only up to 1000 nM. The file gave no such testing range. The greater-than sign established a lower bound on the reported result. For the rest of the story, we would have to go back to the methods. I asked it to remove the inference. My project notes

Those are the moments I design around. A distribution plot takes up space beside a summary. A comparison sign, a unit and a link back to the source all take up space in a crowded table. I’ll give them that space. When two totals disagree, the scientist can settle the arithmetic without taking the assistant’s word for it a second time. Even a perfectly checked enzyme assay still leaves us a long way from a patient on a staircase.

At Anew Labs, I’d want to see what changed on the shortlist

A Shanghai drug discovery company, Anew Labs, published a technical report this year on its engine, AnewDDE. One detail caught my attention. Its pharmaceutical experts built an internal evaluation called PharmBench, with 200 questions across 50 R&D scenarios grounded in experimental evidence. Some questions ask whether an assay itself is sound. After my encounter with the missing compound, I was glad to see that kind of question count towards a model’s score. Anew Labs technical report

The same report describes experimental results changing which candidates are prioritised in the next round. That made me want to see a screen that rarely makes it into a product demo. Put yesterday’s shortlist beside today’s and pick a candidate that moved down. Could a colleague tell which claim began as a model prediction, what the lab had since measured and who changed their mind as a result? A disappointing measurement is worth keeping if it saves the next person from repeating the same bet.

If I could run one product test with the scientists at Anew Labs, I’d ask them to choose a real candidate someone changed their mind about. Give the records from both rounds to a colleague who missed the discussion. Time how long it takes them to find the evidence that changed the decision. Then let them explain, in their own words, why they’d spend the next experiment there. The first moment they have to stop and ask someone for help is where I’d start designing. A benchmark can tell us how a model answered. This little test would tell us whether its evidence survives a handover.

Reading the 98.4 mL again

Time to put the other three groups back in the picture. The phase 2a study randomly assigned 71 people with pulmonary fibrosis to four groups. Seventeen received a placebo. Each of the three treatment groups had eighteen people on different doses or schedules. After twelve weeks, the placebo group averaged 20.3 mL below its baseline. The group taking 60 mg once daily averaged 98.4 mL above its baseline. The 30 mg twice-daily group is easy to overlook. Those participants received the same total amount per day as the 60 mg once-daily group, yet their average gain was 19.7 mL. With eighteen people assigned to each schedule, it’s hard to tell how much of that difference comes from the schedule and how much from who happened to be in the group. Phase 2a paper

Find zero on the chart, then trace the line through the last dot. The 95% confidence interval for that group’s average change runs from 10.9 to 185.9 mL. That’s a wide span. It tells us how imprecisely this small study estimates a group mean. It cannot tell us how much one patient will gain. Estimating the difference between treatment and placebo calls for a comparison between the groups, with its own uncertainty.

The trial had a primary endpoint, the question the investigators had designated in advance. They wanted to know how many participants experienced at least one adverse event after starting treatment. People in all four groups did. Sixteen participants stopped treatment early across the study. Liver-related problems and diarrhoea were among the reasons for stopping. Lung function was a secondary endpoint. Tell this as a story about one encouraging dot on a chart, and those sixteen people quietly drop out of the account. Phase 2a paper

I hope this drug works. The people who join these trials give much more than the time it takes me to read a paper. Being precise about how far the evidence takes us seems a decent way to respect that. In September 2026, Insilico announced that the first participant in its Chinese phase 3 study had received a dose. Its registered primary question concerns the rate of decline in lung capacity over fifty-two weeks. Phase 3 registration

When a headline says AI discovered a drug

I underline the verb, then look for the thing somebody actually measured. Did a protein move up a ranking? Did a chemist make the molecule and test it? Did someone come back a year after their first visit and breathe into the same instrument? Once I find the action, I look for the comparison and the time that elapsed. On this one, I’m waiting for the next breath.
